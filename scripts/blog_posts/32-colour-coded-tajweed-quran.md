A colour-coded Tajweed Quran is a mushaf where certain letters are printed in colour to show which Tajweed rule applies: how long to stretch a vowel, where to make a nasal sound, which letters are silent and where to bounce. It is one of the most helpful tools for beginners, because the rules are visible while you read. This guide explains what the colours usually mean, who benefits most and how to use a colour-coded Quran well.

:::key
- Colours mark **madd (lengthening)**, **ghunnah (nasal sound)**, **silent letters** and other rules such as **qalqalah**.
- Colour systems **vary by publisher**, so check the key printed in your copy.
- Great for **beginners, children and parents** checking a child's reading.
- The colours show **where** a rule applies, but a teacher shows **how** it sounds.
:::

## What Is a Colour-Coded Tajweed Quran?

It is a normal Quran text in the standard Uthmani script, with Tajweed rules highlighted in colour. The words and verses are exactly the same as any other mushaf.

### Why it was created

Tajweed rules are invisible in a plain mushaf. A student must remember every rule and spot it in real time. Colours do the spotting for you, so you can focus on the sound.

### Is it allowed?

Yes. Scholars have accepted these mushafs, because the colours add no words and change nothing in the text. They are similar to the vowel marks and signs added to the mushaf over centuries to help readers.

## What the Colours Usually Mean

Different publishers use different colours. Many popular editions use a system broadly like the one below. Always check the colour key at the front or back of your mushaf.

| Colour family | Usually marks | Examples of the rule |
|---|---|---|
| Red shades | Madd (lengthening), darker for longer | 6 count madd lazim, 4 to 5 count muttasil, 2 count natural madd |
| Green | Ghunnah (nasal sound) | Ikhfa, Idgham with ghunnah, Iqlab, noon and meem with shaddah |
| Blue shades | Heavy ra and qalqalah | Full-mouth ra, the echo on ق ط ب ج د |
| Grey | Silent letters | Hamzat al-wasl, the lam of ٱل before sun letters, unread alif |

### Madd in red

Red shades show how long to stretch a vowel. The darker the red, the longer the madd. Learn the counts in our guide to [madd in Tajweed](/blog/madd-in-tajweed/).

### Ghunnah in green

Green shows where to make the nasal sound for about two counts, including the [noon sakinah and tanween rules](/blog/noon-sakinah-and-tanween-rules/) of Ikhfa, Idgham and Iqlab.

### Qalqalah and silent letters

Blue often marks [qalqalah](/blog/qalqalah-in-tajweed/), the bounce on the five letters. Grey letters are written but not pronounced, a common stumbling point for beginners.

## Who Benefits Most from a Tajweed Quran?

Colour-coded mushafs help some readers more than others.

:::cards
- **Beginners** | Moving from the Qaida to the Quran, colours remind them of rules they just learned.
- **Children** | Bright colours keep attention and make rules feel like a game.
- **Parents** | Even parents who do not know Tajweed can check if a child held a red madd or made a green ghunnah.
- **Adults improving recitation** | Fluent readers who never learned Tajweed can spot rules on every page.
:::

### When to move to a plain mushaf

Eventually, students should read from a normal mushaf, applying rules from memory. Many teachers switch students once they apply the rules without looking at colours, often after a year or so.

### For Hifz students

Memorising always from the same mushaf helps visual memory. Choose one print, colour-coded or plain, and stay with it, as explained in [how to memorise the Quran](/blog/how-to-memorize-quran/).

## How to Use a Colour-Coded Quran Well

Colours are a tool. Used well, they speed up learning.

:::steps
1. **Read the colour key** in your copy before anything else.
2. **Learn one colour at a time,** starting with grey (silent letters) and red (madd).
3. **Read slowly,** pausing at each colour to apply the rule.
4. **Listen to a reciter** while following the colours with your finger.
5. **Recite to a teacher** who checks that your sound matches the colour.
:::

### The limits of colours

Colours cannot teach makharij, the place each letter comes from, or the exact sound of a ghunnah. These come only from listening and correction. See [what Tajweed is](/blog/what-is-tajweed/) and [makharij al-huroof](/blog/makharij-al-huroof/).

:::faq
Q: What do the colours in a Tajweed Quran mean?
A: In many popular colour-coded Quran editions, red shades mark madd or lengthening, with darker red meaning longer, green marks ghunnah or nasal sounds such as Ikhfa and Idgham, blue marks heavy ra and qalqalah, and grey marks silent letters. Colour systems differ between publishers, so always check the colour key printed in your own copy.
Q: Is a colour-coded Quran good for beginners?
A: Yes. A colour-coded Tajweed Quran is very helpful for beginners, because it shows exactly where each rule applies while you read. It helps children moving on from the Noorani Qaida and adults who never learned Tajweed. For best results, use it alongside a teacher who shows you how each rule should sound.
Q: Can I learn Tajweed just from a colour-coded Quran?
A: A colour-coded Quran shows where rules apply, but it cannot teach the correct sound of letters, the right ghunnah or the exact madd length. Those are learned by listening to a qualified teacher and being corrected. Use the colours as a reminder and a teacher for the sound, and your Tajweed will improve much faster.
Q: Is it permissible to read a colour-coded Quran?
A: Yes. Scholars have accepted colour-coded Tajweed mushafs because the colours do not add or change any word of the Quran. They only highlight letters to show recitation rules, similar to the vowel marks and stop signs that were added to the mushaf over the centuries to help people read correctly.
:::
